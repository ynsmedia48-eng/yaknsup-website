Canyon Lake: A Rich History and Paddling Paradise

By Yak N Sup·January 2025·6 min read·Canyon Lake, AZ

Every time you push off from the dock at Canyon Lake Marina, you're floating on a century of history. The canyon walls above you have been witness to Apache raids, military campaigns, the greatest engineering project in Arizona's early history, and a World War II training operation that most people have never heard of.

Canyon Lake is beautiful. But it's also one of the most historically layered places in the American Southwest. Here's the story behind the water.

Before the Dam: The Salt River and the Superstitions

Before Canyon Lake existed, this was the Salt River Canyon — a dramatic gorge cutting through the heart of the Superstition Mountains east of what would become Phoenix. The Salt River was wild, seasonal, and unpredictable. Flash floods were catastrophic. Drought years brought the river to a trickle.

The Apache people had inhabited these mountains for generations. The Superstitions were sacred ground — the mythical home of the Thunder God — and the canyons and ridges were familiar territory for Apache warriors and their families. (The area's darker history with U.S. military operations in these canyons is recounted in our post about the Skeleton Cave Massacre.)

The Salt River Project and the Age of Dams

When Phoenix was founded in 1867, the Salt River Valley held enormous agricultural potential — but only if the river could be controlled. The answer was the Salt River Project, one of the first major federal water reclamation programs in U.S. history.

Starting with Roosevelt Dam in 1911, the Salt River Project built a series of dams along the Salt River and its tributaries to create reliable water storage for the Phoenix valley. Each dam created a reservoir, and each reservoir became part of the integrated water system that made the modern Phoenix metro possible.

  • 1911

    Roosevelt Dam

    The first and largest of the Salt River reservoirs, creating Roosevelt Lake. Declared a National Historic Landmark — the first masonry dam built by the federal government.

  • 1925

    Mormon Flat Dam / Canyon Lake

    Mormon Flat Dam is completed across the Salt River, creating Canyon Lake. Named after the community of Mormon settlers who had farmed the valley downstream.

  • 1927

    Horse Mesa Dam / Apache Lake

    The next dam upstream creates Apache Lake, the most remote and arguably most dramatic of the Salt River reservoirs.

  • 1930

    Stewart Mountain Dam / Saguaro Lake

    The lowest of the chain, closest to Phoenix, creates Saguaro Lake — now the most heavily visited of the four.

💧 The Numbers Behind Canyon Lake

Canyon Lake covers approximately 950 acres at full pool and holds about 57,000 acre-feet of water — enough to supply approximately 173,000 Arizona households for a full year. The lake is about 10 miles long and 1 mile wide at its widest point. The canyon walls that frame the lake rise 500–1,500 feet above the water in places.

World War II: Canyon Lake's Secret Military History

One of the lesser-known chapters of Canyon Lake's history involves World War II and an unlikely military training operation. In the early 1940s, portions of the Salt River lakes were used by the U.S. military for amphibious assault training — practice for the beach landings that would define the Pacific theater of the war.

The terrain around Canyon Lake — steep canyon walls rising directly from the water — created unique training conditions that couldn't easily be replicated elsewhere. The lake's remoteness and the dramatic vertical walls provided cover and realistic obstacles for training exercises.

The full extent of the wartime operations here has never been extensively documented in public records, but local historians and longtime residents have shared accounts of military boats on the lake and troops training in the canyon passages in the early 1940s.

The Superstition Wilderness and the Lost Dutchman

The mountains that frame Canyon Lake are among the most myth-laden in the American West. The Superstition Mountains are home to the legend of the Lost Dutchman's Gold Mine — the story of Jacob Waltz, a German immigrant (the "Dutchman" nickname came from a mispronunciation of "Deutsch") who claimed to have discovered a fabulously rich gold mine somewhere in the Superstitions in the 1870s and 80s.

Waltz took the mine's location to his grave in 1891. Ever since, treasure hunters have been searching the Superstitions for the mine. Dozens have died in the attempt — from dehydration, falls, and in some cases, circumstances that were never fully explained. The legend persists, and every year new people come to the Superstitions convinced they'll be the one to find it.

From a kayak on Canyon Lake, you're looking directly at the terrain where the Lost Dutchman Mine is supposedly hidden. The ridgelines, canyons, and formations that hunters have obsessed over for 130 years are right above you.

Canyon Lake Today

Today, Canyon Lake is managed by the Salt River Project as part of the Phoenix metro's water supply system, while the surrounding land is administered by the Tonto National Forest. Recreation on the lake — boating, kayaking, paddleboarding, swimming, fishing — is overseen by Maricopa County Parks and the Forest Service.

Canyon Lake Marina, where Yak N Sup is based, has operated at the same location since the mid-20th century. It remains one of the few developed access points on the lake — a place where generations of Arizona families have launched boats and started adventures.

When you rent a kayak or paddleboard from us and push off into the lake, you're joining more than 100 years of people who have found this canyon and had their breath taken away. That never gets old.
