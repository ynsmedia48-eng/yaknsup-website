The Skeleton Cave Massacre: A Dark Chapter in Canyon Lake's History

By Yak N Sup·January 2025·4 min read·Canyon Lake, AZ

Canyon Lake is one of the most beautiful places in Arizona. But beauty and history don't always coexist comfortably — and the canyon walls that make this lake so dramatic have witnessed things worth knowing about.

In December 1872, in the Salt River Canyon not far from what is now Canyon Lake Marina, a group of Apache men, women, and children seeking shelter in a cave were ambushed by U.S. military forces. The event — known as the Salt River Canyon Battle or the Skeleton Cave Massacre — is one of the most sobering episodes of the Apache Wars in Arizona Territory.

The Context: The Apache Wars

By the late 1860s and early 1870s, the U.S. Army had launched a systematic military campaign to force the Apache people — who had inhabited the Superstition Mountains and surrounding ranges for generations — onto reservations. General George Crook, placed in command of the Department of Arizona in 1871, launched an aggressive winter campaign in 1872–73 specifically targeting Apache groups who refused relocation.

The Tonto Apache and Yavapai bands who lived in and around the Salt River Canyon had deep roots in this terrain. The canyon walls, the creek passages, the hidden caves — this was home territory. When military pressure intensified in late 1872, a group of Apache (primarily Yavapai, though accounts vary on the exact composition) retreated into a large cave high in the Salt River Canyon wall, believing it to be a safe refuge.

December 28, 1872

On the morning of December 28, 1872, a combined force of U.S. infantry, cavalry, and Apache scouts under Lieutenant William Brown located the cave. The party climbed to a position above the cave opening — a strategic advantage that turned the cave's protected position into a trap.

When soldiers opened fire from the cliff above, bullets ricocheted off the back wall of the cave and back through the crowd of people inside. There was nowhere to go. An estimated 75 to 100 Apache were killed, with the majority being women, children, and elderly. A much smaller number survived by surrendering or escaping through the gunfire.

The cave was named "Skeleton Cave" afterward by the soldiers who found the remains. The name persists to this day.

📍 Where Is Skeleton Cave?

Skeleton Cave is located in the Salt River Canyon, in the area now known as the Tonto National Forest — in the same general region as Canyon Lake. The cave itself is accessible by hiking via the Salt River Canyon trail system, though the terrain is rugged and the hike is not trivial. It is not visible from the lake surface, but the canyon walls you paddle alongside when you're on Canyon Lake are the same geological formation.

Why This History Matters

The Salt River Canyon and Canyon Lake sit on land that was home to the Yavapai, Tonto Apache, and other Indigenous peoples for thousands of years before the military campaigns of the 1870s. The Superstition Mountains were not just terrain — they were sacred ground, a cultural landscape woven into the identity of the people who lived there.

The construction of Mormon Flat Dam in 1925, which created Canyon Lake, permanently changed the canyon. The water that now fills the valley was once dry canyon floor — a different landscape, but the same canyon walls.

When you paddle Canyon Lake today, the canyon around you is the same canyon where all of this happened. The walls haven't changed. The rock formations that provided shelter and escape routes for Apache families are still there, above the waterline. The creek passages — La Barge Creek, Tortilla Creek — are the same drainages that those families knew.

Understanding this history doesn't diminish the beauty of Canyon Lake. But it does add depth to it. The Superstitions have layers — geological, historical, cultural — that extend far beyond what's visible from the water. Knowing some of those layers is part of knowing this place.

The Yavapai and Fort McDowell Today

The Yavapai Nation did not disappear after the campaigns of the 1870s. The Fort McDowell Yavapai Nation is today a federally recognized tribe whose reservation sits along the Verde River, about 25 miles from Canyon Lake. The tribe operates Fort McDowell Yavapai Nation Casino Resort and maintains an active cultural and governmental presence in the region.

The Tonto Apache, related to but distinct from the Yavapai, are represented today by the Tonto Apache Tribe of Arizona, based in Payson. Their ancestral territory encompassed the Tonto Basin and much of the terrain surrounding Canyon Lake.

These communities continue to live in Arizona and maintain connections to the land their ancestors called home.
